01

A supply chain
needs a place.

Pax Silica is a US State Department–led initiative, begun in December 2025, to secure technology supply chains: critical minerals, semiconductors, AI and advanced manufacturing. The name joins the Latin pax (peace) with silica, the raw material of silicon.

The Philippines signed on 17 April 2026 as the 13th signatory. With it came the first physical project under the pact: a 4,000-acre “economic security zone” that officials call the first “AI-native industrial acceleration hub”, inside New Clark City in Capas, Tarlac, part of the Luzon Economic Corridor.

According to Human Rights Watch, the Philippine government provides the land while investors, including US companies, finance infrastructure and facilities. The government says the hub will bring investment, jobs and local mineral processing. Critics say it is being built on land that people already occupy.

Status · 19 Nov 2026

The US–Philippines framework agreement on the hub is still unsigned. In early November the Palace said Manila was waiting on a US response to a draft.

  1. Dec 2025

    Pax Silica Declaration launched in Washington with the US, Japan, South Korea, Singapore, the UK, Australia and Israel among first signatories.

  2. 16–17 Apr 2026

    The Philippines joins; Trade Undersecretary Ceferino Rodolfo signs. The 4,000-acre hub in New Clark City is announced.

  3. 25–26 Jun 2026

    Second Pax Silica Summit. The EU and others sign, bringing the total to 24 signatories.

  4. Jul 2026

    BCDA letters reach Aeta households in Sapang Kawayan, saying their land will be “affected”, without mentioning the hub. On 23 July BCDA announces the hub will be built inside New Clark City.

  5. Aug 2026

    The Network Opposed to Pax Silica launches. The National Commission on Indigenous Peoples (NCIP) visits Aeta Hungey communities to hear their concerns.

  6. 11 Nov 2026

    BCDA tells the House the proposed relocation site is Sitio Binyayan, about 3 km from Sapang Kawayan.

  7. 16 Nov 2026

Seven places, drawn to the same scale

Central Park runs about 4 km end to end — nearly the same as one side of the hub — but covers only a fifth of it. The whole of Vatican City would fit more than thirty times over.

Even after stacking all seven places inside the hub, close to a sixth of it is still empty.

Or, in football pitches

Each block is one football pitch (105 × 68 m). Laid side by side, the pitches would fill a square about 4 kilometres across.

Share of New Clark City

17% rest of New Clark City

New Clark City is commonly cited as about 9,450 ha. The hub (≈1,619 ha) would take roughly one-sixth of it. The Palace has also said around 6,000 ha of the wider area is reserved for forest, green space and upland farming.

How these numbers were calculated

4,000 acres × 0.404686 = 1,618.7 ha (officials round to 1,618–1,620). Side of an equal-area square = √16.19 km² ≈ 4.02 km. A pitch of 105 × 68 m = 0.714 ha, so 1,618.7 ÷ 0.714 ≈ 2,267. Landmark areas are public, rounded figures: Central Park 341 ha, UP Diliman campus 493 ha, Monaco 202 ha, Hyde Park 142 ha, Intramuros 67 ha, Rizal Park 58 ha, Vatican City 49 ha.

03

Someone
already lives here.

Inside the planned footprint is Sapang Kawayan, a village of roughly 200 Aeta households of the Aeta Hungey, in Barangay Aranguren, Capas. Independent geographers supporting the Aeta have mapped the proposed hub and place the village inside it; another community, the Ayta Mag-Antsi at Sitio Manabayukan, sits just outside.

The Aeta Hungey have sought recognition of their ancestral domain since 1994, and refiled in 1999 after the Indigenous Peoples’ Rights Act took effect. They still hold no title. BCDA’s president told a Senate hearing the Aeta were living on government land. This is the core of the dispute: the land is state-administered, and the people have claimed it for decades.

Nearby, in Sitio Binyayan (Barangay O’Donnell), BCDA confirmed 92 “affected persons”, mostly farmers who till land without title. They report offers of ₱300,000 per hectare, and say they were given 10 to 30 days to respond.

What residents and rights groups say
  • Aeta Hungey leaders say they learned of the project through social media and received no prior consultation.
  • They say BCDA officials told them orally they would be relocated by December, to homes of about 30 m².
  • The proposed relocation site is about 3 km away and, per HRW, has no access to the streams they use for water.
  • Residents describe burial grounds, a church, farms and water sources that would be lost. One farmer: Money is temporary; land is forever.
  • HRW says available information indicates free, prior and informed consent was never obtained.

How many people? The numbers don’t agree.

Log scale. These figures measure different things (households vs. “affected persons” vs. all people in or near the footprint), so they are not like-for-like. Neither the Palace nor BCDA accepts the highest estimate.

What the government says
  • BCDA’s former president said about 40 Aeta homes, around 200 people, would be “pushed a little”.
  • The Palace says relocation sites and financial assistance will be provided if displacement is necessary, and that farmers will not be affected.
  • BCDA says it welcomes Senate oversight and is meeting the Capas local government. The Capas municipal government has welcomed the project’s economic potential, while saying communities should be consulted.
The rights framework
  • The 1997 IPRA gives Indigenous peoples rights to lands held “since time immemorial” and requires free, prior and informed consent (FPIC) for projects on them.
  • NCIP revised its FPIC guidelines in May 2026. Critics say the changes shorten the time communities have to reach consensus.
  • The NCIP says it is monitoring the project and will act to protect Aeta Hungey rights under existing law.
  • Caritas Philippines’ president, Bishop Gerardo Alminaza, says he is not against technology but wants dialogue and safeguards.

History rhymes: when the US ran Clark Air Base, many Aeta were displaced to make room for it. Today’s plan is a new chapter on the same ground.

04

What a hub
drinks and eats.

Water

Scientists in the Network Opposed to Pax Silica warn that water demand would peak in the dry season (December to May), the same months farmers need irrigation. The Bangot River is a lifeline for Aeta livelihoods near Capas.

No public water-use plan for the hub has been published in the sources reviewed.

Power

One reported estimate says a facility running continuously could need around 26.3 TWh a year. For comparison, the entire Philippine service sector used about 28 TWh in 2024.

BCDA’s chief has pointed to solar potential (“300 days of sunshine”). The estimate is a projection, not a measured figure.

Land & waste

Farmer groups such as KMP warn of land conversion and weaker food security. Others have raised waste handling and the thorium that can accompany rare-earth processing.

Government position: about 6,000 ha around the hub is set aside for forest, green space and upland farming.

Supporters argue

Processing minerals at home rather than exporting raw ore, plus technology transfer and jobs in a country with more than three million unemployed. A pro-hub analyst says benefits outweigh risks, while criticising the government for explaining them poorly.

Farmers worry

Many say farming is the only work they know and doubt they have the skills for the jobs promised. A 30-day offer on land they have tilled for generations is not a career change.

Labor critics

Think tank IBON argues the Philippines would sit at the labor-intensive back end of the chain, where history shows wage pressure and weak labor protections.

Analysts note

As the physical home of a flagship project, the Philippines would carry risks other signatories — whose roles are mostly financial or policy — do not, including becoming a target in a tech-security rivalry.

06

What is
still open.

  • Unsigned
    The framework agreement. Still a draft awaiting a US response as of early November; no definite signing date.
  • Pending
    Public consultation. The Capas mayor and the Barangay O’Donnell head asked BCDA for one. “To this day, we are still waiting,” the barangay head told HRW.
  • Disputed
    Free, prior and informed consent. HRW says it was not obtained; NCIP says it is monitoring and has held dialogues with Aeta communities.
  • Since 1994
    The Aeta Hungey ancestral domain title. Claimed for three decades, still not granted.
  • Proposed
    Relocation. A site 3 km away, reportedly without access to the streams the community uses.
  • Contested
    The scale of displacement. From about 200 people (BCDA) to hundreds (HRW) to 20,000 (protest groups’ claim, disputed by the Palace).
